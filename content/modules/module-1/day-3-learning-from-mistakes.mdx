> *"GPT doesn't learn because someone teaches it the answers. It learns because it repeatedly realizes it was wrong."*

---

## The Problem

So far, we have:

1. A cleaned dataset
2. Token IDs
3. A neural network
4. Billions of random parameters

Everything is finally ready.

Now comes the most important moment.

The model must answer its very first question.

Suppose the training sentence is:

```text
The sky is ____
```

The correct next word is `blue`.

But remember — the model has never learned English.

What do you think it predicts?

Probably something completely wrong.

And that's exactly where learning begins.

---

## Intuition

Imagine asking a first-grade student:

> 8 × 7 = ?

The student answers: `42`

Wrong.

You immediately tell them:

> The correct answer is 56.

Next time, their chances improve.

Learning happens because:

```text
Prediction
↓
Mistake
↓
Correction
↓
Better Prediction
```

GPT follows exactly the same philosophy.

---

## The Prediction Pipeline

```text
Input Tokens
↓
Neural Network
↓
Prediction Scores
↓
Probabilities
↓
Predicted Word
```

Every training example follows this pipeline.

---

## Step 1 — Prediction

The token IDs enter the neural network.

Inside the network, billions of mathematical operations happen.

Eventually, the model produces one score for every word in its vocabulary.

Imagine the vocabulary contains only five words:

```text
blue      → 8.1
green     → 6.2
dog       → 0.5
apple     → 1.8
computer  → 3.9
```

These raw scores are called **Logits**.

The neural network computes the raw prediction scores (logits) using:

<MathBlock title="Linear Transformation">
  $$z = Wx + b$$
</MathBlock>

where **x** is the input representation, **W** is the learnable weight matrix, **b** is the bias, and **z** is the output logits.

> We'll study this computation in detail when we learn about transformer layers. For now, just remember that this equation produces the logits.

At this stage they are **not probabilities**. They are simply scores representing how much the model currently prefers each possible next token.

---

## Logits

Think of logits as contestants in a competition.

A higher score means more likely to win.

But the scores themselves don't tell us actual probabilities.

For that, we need another step.

---

## Step 2 — Softmax

Softmax converts logits into probabilities.

Example:

```text
blue      → 0.78
green     → 0.14
computer  → 0.05
apple     → 0.02
dog       → 0.01
```

Now everything makes sense. The probabilities add up to `1.0` (100%).

The model can now confidently choose the most probable next token.

Softmax converts the logits into probabilities using:

<MathBlock title="Softmax Function">
  $$P(i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j} e^{z_j}}$$
</MathBlock>

where **zᵢ** is the logit of token *i* and **P(i)** is the probability assigned to token *i*.

> This ensures that every probability lies between **0 and 1**, and together they sum to **1**.

---

## Step 3 — Compare with the Correct Answer

Suppose the correct answer was `blue`.

If the model predicts `computer`, it made a mistake.

But how bad was that mistake?

We need a way to measure it.

---

## Step 4 — Loss

Loss is simply a number that tells us: **How wrong was the prediction?**

```text
Small Loss  →  Good prediction
Large Loss  →  Poor prediction
```

Think of loss like exam marks:

```text
100%  →  Excellent
35%   →  Needs improvement
```

The model uses loss as feedback after every prediction.

A commonly used loss function for language models is **Cross Entropy Loss**:

<MathBlock title="Cross Entropy Loss">
  $$L = -\log(P(\text{correct token}))$$
</MathBlock>

where **L** is the loss and **P(correct token)** is the probability assigned to the correct next token.

> A lower loss means the model predicted the correct answer with higher confidence.

---

## Step 5 — Backpropagation

Now comes the clever part.

The model asks itself:

> Which parameters caused this mistake?

Backpropagation traces the error backwards through the network.

It discovers:

- which connections contributed most
- which parameters should change
- how much they should change

It does **not** rebuild the network. It simply calculates how each parameter should be adjusted.

---

## Step 6 — Gradient Descent

Backpropagation tells us which parameters should change.

Gradient Descent performs the update.

Imagine standing on a mountain. Your goal is to reach the valley.

Every step you take moves you downhill. Eventually, you reach the lowest point.

Training works similarly. The model keeps adjusting parameters to reduce the loss.

Gradient Descent updates the parameters using:

<MathBlock title="Gradient Descent Update">
  $$w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \eta \nabla L$$
</MathBlock>

where **w** is the model parameter, **η** is the learning rate, and **∇L** is the gradient of the loss.

> This equation simply tells the model how to adjust its parameters to reduce future mistakes.

---

## Complete Training Cycle

```text
Input Sentence
↓
Tokenization
↓
Neural Network
↓
Logits
↓
Softmax
↓
Prediction
↓
Loss
↓
Backpropagation
↓
Gradient Descent
↓
Updated Parameters
↓
Repeat
```

This entire cycle happens billions of times.

---

## Why Training Takes So Long

One prediction doesn't teach much. Neither do a thousand.

GPT repeats this process on enormous amounts of text — sometimes billions of examples, trillions of tokens, weeks or months of continuous computation.

Each prediction improves the parameters just a tiny amount.

Knowledge emerges from these tiny improvements accumulating over time.

---

## Worked Example

Imagine learning basketball.

First shot: Miss. Coach corrects you.

Second shot: Closer. Coach corrects you again.

Hundreds of practice sessions later, your shooting becomes natural.

You didn't become skilled after one correction.

You improved through thousands of small adjustments.

GPT learns exactly the same way.

---

## Common Misconceptions

**GPT memorizes every sentence.**

No. It learns statistical patterns, not exact copies.

**Loss is the mistake itself.**

No. Loss is only a measurement of how large the mistake was.

**Backpropagation changes the architecture.**

No. The architecture stays the same. Only the parameters change.

**One prediction teaches the model.**

No. Learning requires billions of prediction–correction cycles.

## Key Takeaways

- GPT learns by making predictions.
- Raw prediction scores are called logits.
- Softmax converts logits into probabilities.
- Loss measures how wrong the prediction was.
- Backpropagation finds which parameters caused the mistake.
- Gradient Descent updates those parameters.
- Repeating this cycle billions of times creates intelligence.
