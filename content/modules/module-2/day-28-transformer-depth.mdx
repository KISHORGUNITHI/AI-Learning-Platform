# Why One Transformer Block Isn't Enough

## Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to answer:

- Why does GPT need many Transformer blocks?
- What happens to a token representation as it moves upward through the stack?
- What is a hidden state?
- Why does each layer produce a better representation rather than creating a new token?
- Why do different layers learn different kinds of information?
- Why does stacking help the model build richer contextual representations?
- Why can the tensor shape stay constant while the information inside it changes?

---

# The Question

We already know what happens inside one individual Transformer block:

<FlowDiagram
  title="Inside One Transformer Block"
  steps={[
    "Input Tensor [S × D]",
    "Pre-Layer Normalization",
    "Causal Multi-Head Attention",
    "Residual Connection (+ Input)",
    "Pre-Layer Normalization",
    "Feed-Forward Network (FFN)",
    "Residual Connection (+ MHA Output)",
    "Output Hidden State [S × D]"
  ]}
/>

So a natural question arises:

> **If one Transformer block already performs multi-head attention, non-linear feed-forward expansion, residual connections, and layer normalization, why does GPT repeat this block 12, 24, 48, 96, or more times?**

The central answer:

<KeyInsight title="The Core Insight on Depth">
One Transformer block performs <strong>one single stage of transformation and refinement</strong>. Stacking many blocks allows the representation to be progressively transformed, composed, and enriched.
</KeyInsight>

# Imagine a Human Reading a Paragraph

Suppose someone asks you to read a challenging paragraph once and immediately explain its deepest themes.

- **Reading once** may give you the basic dictionary meaning of words and surface facts.
- **Reading again** helps you notice structural connections, pronoun references, and clause relationships.
- **Repeated refinement** produces a rich, coherent understanding of nuance, subtext, and overarching intent.

<FlowDiagram
  title="The Refinement Process"
  steps={[
    "Read Once → Extract basic word meanings & surface tokens",
    "Read Again → Notice grammatical links & entity relationships",
    "Repeated Refinement → Synthesize intent, abstract nuance & prediction context"
  ]}
/>

A deep Transformer operates on a related engineering principle: **each block receives the representation produced by the previous block and refines it further**.

---

# The Key Idea: Stacking

A Transformer **does not** repeatedly create new tokens at each layer.

Instead:

> **The same token positions are repeatedly transformed and enriched with increasingly sophisticated contextual information.**

Here is how information flows vertically through the network:

<TransformerStackVisualizer
  title="Vertical Information Flow Across Stacked Transformer Blocks"
  caption="Tokens enter as initial embeddings. Each block refines the representation, preserving tensor shape until the final hidden state projects to logits."
/>

Each block receives the previous block's output tensor as its own input:

<MathBlock title="Layer-by-Layer Transformations">

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_0 &= \text{Embedding}(X) + \text{PositionalEncoding} \\
H_1 &= \text{TransformerBlock}_1(H_0) \\
H_2 &= \text{TransformerBlock}_2(H_1) \\
&\;\;\vdots \\
H_N &= \text{TransformerBlock}_N(H_{N-1})
\end{aligned}
$$

</MathBlock>

# What Actually Changes From Block to Block?

A common misconception is:

> *"If the architecture of every Transformer block is identical, isn't the model just repeating the exact same calculation over and over?"*

**No.**

While the structural blueprint (Attention $\to$ Add $\to$ Norm $\to$ FFN $\to$ Add $\to$ Norm) is identical across blocks:

<KeyInsight title="Distinct Learned Parameters">
Each Transformer block has its own completely independent set of learned parameters:
$$\text{Block}_1 \neq \text{Block}_2 \neq \text{Block}_3 \dots \neq \text{Block}_N$$
Each layer possesses its own $W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O, W_1, W_2, \gamma, \beta$. Therefore, every layer performs a uniquely trained mathematical transformation on the hidden states it receives.
</KeyInsight>

---

# The Tensor Shape Stays the Same

This is one of the most foundational principles of modern Transformer engineering.

Suppose we process a sequence of 4 tokens with an embedding hidden dimension of 768:

$$\text{Sequence Length } S = 4$$
$$\text{Hidden Dimension } D = 768$$

The tensor shape entering and exiting every block is:

$$\text{Shape} = [4 \times 768]$$

<ShapeVsRepresentationVisualizer
  title="Shape Invariance vs Representation Evolution"
  caption="Dimensions stay fixed at [4 × 768] for seamless tensor passing, while representation content evolves from R₀ to R_N."
/>

<MathBlock
  title="Tensor Shape Compatibility Through the Stack"
  caption="Constant tensor dimensions ensure the output of any block can serve directly as the input to the subsequent block without reshaping."
>

$$H_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 768} \xrightarrow{\text{Block}_1} H_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 768} \xrightarrow{\text{Block}_2} H_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 768} \xrightarrow{\dots} H_N \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 768}$$

</MathBlock>

Imagine a student who begins studying a textbook on mathematics:

- After studying 12 chapters, their understanding of mathematics has deepened enormously.
- **Yet the student has not physically grown 12 times larger or gained additional heads.**

Similarly:

> **A Transformer hidden state keeps the exact same dimensional footprint ($[S \times 768]$), while the information density, relational depth, and contextual nuance encoded in its numbers expand dramatically.**

# What Is a Hidden State?

Inside neural networks, intermediate representations are called **hidden states**.

<KeyInsight title="Definition: Hidden State">
A <strong>hidden state</strong> is an intermediate continuous numerical vector produced inside the model that encapsulates what the network currently understands and represents about a token at that specific stage of processing.
</KeyInsight>

For a sequence of tokens, the complete hidden state at any layer is a 2D matrix (or 3D batch tensor):

$$\text{Hidden State Matrix } H \in \mathbb{R}^{\text{sequence\_length} \times \text{hidden\_size}}$$

<HiddenStateTensorVisualizer
  title="Interactive Hidden State Tensor Inspection [4 × 768]"
  caption="Each row represents one token in the sequence. Each column is a continuous feature coordinate. Explore how activations change across layers."
/>

Each row $i$ is a 768-dimensional continuous vector:

$$\mathbf{h}_i = [d_0, d_1, d_2, \dots, d_{767}]$$

<Warning title="No Single Dimension Holds One Human Concept">
Never assume that dimension $d_{42}$ represents "is it an animal" and dimension $d_{99}$ represents "is it blue". The representation is <strong>distributed</strong> across all 768 coordinates simultaneously.
</Warning>

---

# Where Do Hidden States Exist?

Hidden states exist at every boundary between layers:

- $H_0$: After embedding lookup and positional encoding addition
- $H_1$: Output of Transformer Block 1
- $H_2$: Output of Transformer Block 2
- $H_3$: Output of Transformer Block 3
- $\dots$
- $H_N$: Output of Final Transformer Block $N$ (Final Hidden State)

# After Transformer Block 1

At the input layer, `"bank"` only possesses its static dictionary embedding. It could refer to a river bank or a financial institution.

After passing through **Transformer Block 1**:
- Multi-Head Attention allows `"bank"` to attend to its immediate local neighbors (`"the"`, `"into"`).
- The residual connection preserves its baseline word identity while adding local context.
- The output vector $\mathbf{h}_1$ is still positioned at index 3 with 768 floats, but it now represents a localized phrase unit.

---

# After Transformer Block 2

Block 2 receives $\mathbf{h}_1$ as its input:
- Attention heads in Block 2 connect `"bank"` to the transitive verb `"deposited"` and noun `"money"`.
- The Feed-Forward Network non-linearly suppresses features related to rivers and geographical slopes, amplifying financial institution features.
- The representation is now structurally bound to the predicate of the sentence.

---

# After Many Blocks

As the token representation continues through Blocks 3, 4, 5, up to Block $N$:
- It integrates discourse-level intent, sentence structure, and tone.
- By the final block, the vector for `"bank"` is no longer a generic dictionary lookup—it is a hyper-specific, context-saturated representation ready to inform subsequent predictions.

# Representation Evolution

Empirical research on deep language models reveals that layers exhibit distinct representation tendencies:

<LayerHierarchyVisualizer
  title="Layer Hierarchy: Early → Middle → Late Representations"
  caption="Different depths in the Transformer stack specialize in distinct linguistic and semantic transformations."
/>

## Early Layers
Early layers (e.g., Layers 1–4) focus on low-level, local, and surface patterns:
- Subword assembly and spelling features
- Punctuation cues and sentence delimiters
- Immediate n-gram and phrase-level pairings

## Middle Layers
Middle layers (e.g., Layers 5–8) build structured linguistic relationships:
- Part-of-speech roles and grammatical syntax
- Subject-verb agreements across long spans
- Coreference resolution (e.g., resolving `"it"` to the appropriate prior noun)

## Late Layers
Late layers (e.g., Layers 9–12+) synthesize high-level semantic abstractions:
- Topic, discourse intent, and world knowledge
- Complex reasoning patterns
- Final feature alignment for next-token vocabulary projection

<Tip title="Conceptual Progression, Not Rigid Hardware Partitions">
This division is a continuous <strong>emergent tendency</strong> learned through optimization, not a set of rigid boundaries hardcoded into the network architecture.
</Tip>

# Why Depth Matters

Consider evaluating an arithmetic expression with nested operations:

$$5 \times (7 + 3) - 10$$

To solve this, you naturally break the computation into sequential stages:

<FlowDiagram
  title="Sequential Composition of Operations"
  steps={[
    "Step 1: Evaluate inner parenthesis → (7 + 3) = 10",
    "Step 2: Multiply by factor → 5 × 10 = 50",
    "Step 3: Subtract offset → 50 - 10 = 40 (Final Answer)"
  ]}
/>

While language processing is far more complex than simple arithmetic, the core principle is identical: **complex cognitive and semantic transformations cannot be resolved in a single flat step**.

Depth provides the sequential computational budget required for iterative refinement.

---

# Why Stacking Increases Model Capacity

Each block applies a parameterized non-linear transformation:

$$H_l = \mathcal{T}_l(H_{l-1}; \theta_l)$$

When we stack $N$ blocks, the overall network computes a deep composition of functions:

$$H_N = (\mathcal{T}_N \circ \mathcal{T}_{N-1} \circ \dots \circ \mathcal{T}_2 \circ \mathcal{T}_1)(H_0)$$

Because each $\theta_l$ contains independent learned weights:
- The function space expressible by $N$ composed blocks is exponentially richer than a single block.
- Residual connections guarantee that gradients can flow directly from $\mathcal{T}_N$ back to $\mathcal{T}_1$ without vanishing during backpropagation.

---

# Who Decides How Many Blocks GPT Has?

The number of Transformer blocks is an **architectural hyperparameter** chosen by the AI model architect prior to training.

The architect balances:
- **Depth (N Blocks)**: Determines sequential reasoning and representational capacity.
- **Width (d_model Hidden Dimension)**: Determines how much information each vector can hold.
- **Attention Heads (H)**: Determines how many independent relationships can be attended to simultaneously.
- **Compute and Memory Budget**: Deeper models require more GPU memory, FLOPs, and training time.

### Examples Across Real GPT Architectures

| Model | Stack Depth (N Blocks) | Hidden Size (d_model) | Attention Heads | Total Parameters |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **GPT-2 Small** | 12 blocks | 768 | 12 heads | 117 Million |
| **GPT-2 Medium** | 24 blocks | 1024 | 16 heads | 345 Million |
| **GPT-2 Large** | 36 blocks | 1280 | 20 heads | 774 Million |
| **GPT-2 XL** | 48 blocks | 1600 | 25 heads | 1.5 Billion |
| **GPT-3** | 96 blocks | 12,288 | 96 heads | 175 Billion |

<Tip title="Takeaway on Model Size">
Do not memorize these numbers. Understand that model depth is an architectural choice that determines how many sequential refinement stages representations pass through.
</Tip>

# One More Important Insight

A single Transformer block does not need to "understand everything" on its own.

Instead:

> **The model distributes computation across depth. Early computation builds useful local building blocks. Middle computation constructs relational networks. Late computation synthesizes those networks into abstract understanding.**

The intelligence of a deep Transformer is not located inside any single block. It **emerges from the composition of many stacked transformations**.

# Key Takeaways

- **GPT stacks many Transformer blocks** because one stage of transformation is not enough for complex multi-step computation.
- **Each block receives the previous block's representation** and passes an enriched hidden state upward.
- **Each block has its own independent learned parameters** ($W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O, W_1, W_2, \gamma, \beta$).
- **Transformer blocks refine representations rather than creating new tokens**.
- **A hidden state is an intermediate representation** inside the model that captures what the network currently represents about the input.
- **A token's representation can evolve substantially** from the embedding layer to the final layer (same token position, increasingly rich vector).
- **Early, middle, and late layers show different learned representations** (local surface patterns → syntax → semantics & prediction).
- **Tensor shape remains constant ([S × D])** while the information encoded in the tensor becomes progressively richer.
- **Model depth is an architectural choice** (e.g., GPT-2 Small: 12 blocks, GPT-2 Medium: 24 blocks, GPT-3: 96 blocks).
- **The final hidden state $H_N$ is passed to the output projection** to produce vocabulary logits for next-token prediction.

## Questions That Changed My Understanding

**Q: If one Transformer block already has Attention and FFN, why isn't one enough?**

A single block performs one stage of refinement. Stacking multiple blocks allows representations to become progressively richer through sequential compositions of non-linear transformations ($f(g(h(x)))$).

**Q: Does every block create a new token?**

No. The tokens remain in their exact sequence positions from input to output. Transformer blocks only transform continuous numerical hidden representations.

**Q: What actually changes from one block to the next?**

The numerical values inside the hidden state tensor change continuously as each layer's attention and feed-forward networks apply their own learned parameters.

**Q: What is a hidden state?**

A hidden state is an intermediate representation produced inside the model that captures what the model currently represents about the input. For a sequence, it is a tensor of shape `[sequence_length × hidden_size]`.

**Q: Why can the tensor shape remain constant while the representation changes?**

Tensor shape defines the geometric container (`[4 × 768]`), which stays compatible so the output of one block can directly feed into the next. The numbers inside the container mutate and become contextually richer.

**Q: Why might early and late layers represent different kinds of information?**

Early layers attend to local, surface features (spelling, word identity, punctuation). Middle layers build structured syntactic dependencies (grammar, subject-object roles). Late layers synthesize high-level semantics, discourse intent, and prediction-relevant features.

**Q: Who decides how many Transformer blocks a GPT model should have?**

The AI model architect decides the number of blocks as an architectural choice, balancing representational capacity, compute budget, and training stability.

**Q: Why does stacking increase the model's representational capacity?**

Each block contributes independent learned parameters and sequential refinement steps, allowing the network to express exponentially richer functions than a single shallow transformation.
